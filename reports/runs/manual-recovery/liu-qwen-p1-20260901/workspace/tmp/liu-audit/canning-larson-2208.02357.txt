ON THE CHOW AND COHOMOLOGY RINGS OF MODULI SPACES OF
STABLE CUR VES
SAMIR CANNING AND HANNAH LARSON
Abstract. In this paper, we ask: for which ( g, n) is the rational Chow or cohomology ring
of Mg,n generated by tautological classes? This question has been fully answered in genus
0 by Keel (the Chow and cohomology rings are tautological for all n [35]) and genus 1 by
Belorousski (the rings are tautological if and only if n ≤ 10 [5]). For g ≥ 2, work of van
Zelm [54] shows the Chow and cohomology rings are not tautological once 2 g + n ≥ 24,
leaving finitely many open cases. Here, we prove that the Chow and cohomology rings of
Mg,n are isomorphic and generated by tautological classes for g = 2 and n ≤ 9 and for
3 ≤ g ≤ 7 and 2 g + n ≤ 14. For such ( g, n), this implies that the tautological ring is
Gorenstein and Mg,n has polynomial point count.
1. Introduction
In his landmark 1983 paper, Mumford proposed the study of the rational Chow rings
of the moduli space of curves Mg and its Deligne–Mumford compactification Mg in low
genus, and began the project by determining A∗(M2) [40]. In 1990, Faber determined
A∗(M3) [25]. Since then, substantial progress has been made for the open moduli spaces,
eventually determining A∗(Mg) for g ≤ 9 [12, 26, 32, 44]. The aim of this paper is to bring
progress to the more challenging problem of determining A∗(Mg). In particular, for g ≤ 7,
we will prove A∗(Mg) is generated by tautological classes and the cycle class map is an
isomorphism. Thus, the ring is algorithmically determinable, as we explain in Section 1.4.
Due to the nature of its boundary, the first step in pursuing results for the compactification
Mg is to consider all the moduli spaces Mg,n of n-pointed stable curves of genus g. Let
R∗(Mg,n) ⊆ A∗(Mg,n) be the subring generated by tautological classes (see Definition 2.1).
Question 1.1. For which (g, n) do we have A∗(Mg,n) = R∗(Mg,n) ?
Our answers to Question 1.1 will also be used to give answers to its analogue in cohomology,
which in turn have several applications. The tautological ring in cohomology RH ∗(Mg,n) ⊆
H ∗(Mg,n) is defined as the image of R∗(Mg,n) under the cycle class map.
Question 1.2. For which (g, n) do we have H ∗(Mg,n) = RH ∗(Mg,n)?

Remark 1.3 (Integral coefficients). Determining the Chow ring of Mg,n with integral co-
efficients is a much more subtle problem. The cases where the integral Chow ring is known
are M0,n by Keel in 1992 [35]; M1,1 by Edidin–Graham in 1998 [19]; M2 by E. Larson [38]
in 2021; M1,2 and M2,1 by Di Lorenzo, Pernice, and Vistoli in 2021 [18].
There are also cases where results are known in cohomology but not in Chow. Much of
the previous work computing H ∗(Mg,n) has used point counting. The number of points
#Mg,n(Fq) is defined as the sum over isomorphism classes of Fq points, weighted by size
of the automorphism group. We say Mg,n has polynomial point count if there exists a
polynomial P with integer coefficients such that # Mg,n(Fq) = P (q) for all prime powers q.
If H ∗(Mg,n) = RH ∗(Mg,n) then Mg,n has polynomial point count (see Proposition 3.15). On
the other hand, if Mg,n has polynomial point count, then all odd cohomology vanishes, and
knowing the polynomial determines the dimensions of all cohomology groups. Previously,
Mg,n was shown to have polynomial point count (and the polynomial determined) for
⊤ g = 2: n ≤ 7 by Bergstr¨ om in 2009 [7];
⊤ g = 3: n ≤ 1 by Getzler–Looijenga in 1999 [29]; n ≤ 5 by Bergstr¨ om in 2008 [6];
⊤ g = 4: n = 0 by Bergstr¨ om and Tommasi in 2006 [8]; 1≤ n ≤ 3 by Bergstr¨ om, Faber
and Payne in 2022 [10].
Upon completing this manuscript, the authors learned that Begstr¨ om and Faber have im-
proved the first two cases above to g = 2, n ≤ 9 and g = 3, n ≤ 7 [9].
Having polynomial point count does not rule out the possibility of non-tautological classes
in even degrees, but with some additional work, it is known that all cohomology is tautolog-
ical in the following cases:
▼ g = 2: n ≤ 9 by work of Petersen and Tommasi in 2016 [46, 47], which proved all
even cohomology is tautological for g = 2 and n <20.
▼ g = 3: n ≤ 1 and g = 4: n = 0 by Schmitt and van Zelm in 2020 [51].
In the remaining genus 3 and 4 cases where polynomial point count is known, it is expected
that all cohomology is tautological. While computer calculations in low codimension support
this expectation, confirming it by computer seems beyond present techniques (see [10, Re-
mark 1.8]). One consequence of our theorem will be confirmation that these polynomial
point counts, as well as many new ones, arise because all cohomology is tautological.
Meanwhile, non-tautological classes have been discovered in the following cases:
× g = 1: n ≥ 11. The weight 12 discriminant cusp form gives rise to a holomor-

Ro ˘ ıtman [50], existence of a holomorphic form implies that the group of zero-cycles

× g = 2: n ≥ 14 and g = 3: n ≥ 15. Similarly, there are known holomorphic differential

1.3. Overview of the new techniques. We prove Theorem 1.4 with a novel method,
which allows us to take advantage of the flexibility of excision in Chow, yet still obtain
results in cohomology at the end. The key idea is prove our spaces have the Chow–K¨ unneth
generation Property (CKgP), which is a way of making up for the lack of a K¨ unneth formula
in Chow. We say X has the CKgP if for all Y , there is a surjection
A∗(X) ⊗ A∗(Y ) → A∗(X × Y ).
Below are some important observations about the CKgP, which pave the way for our
approach. Each italicized statement is not difficult to establish (and some have already
appeared in the literature [3, 52]), but they combine into a very powerful tool when applied
to moduli spaces of curves:
(1) The CKgP plays well with stratifications, products, and finite group quotients. Thus,
using the inductive nature of its boundary, we can reduce showing Mg,n has the
CKgP and A∗(Mg,n) = R∗(Mg,n) to showing the open moduli spaces Mg′,n′ have
the CKgP and A∗(Mg′,n′) = R∗(Mg′,n′) for g′ ≤ g and 2g′ + n′ ≤ 2g + n (as explained
in Section 4).
(2) If X → Y is proper and surjective, and X has the CKgP, then Y has the CKgP .
Thus, to attack each piece Mg′,n′, we can pass from studying its gonality strata to
studying Hurwitz spaces of covers with marked points (see Sections 8–10).
(3) If U ⊂ X is open and X has the CKgP, then U has the CKgP. Often, we can construct
a moduli space of marked curves (or curves with a map to P1 or P2) as a quotient
of affine space minus some discriminant locus. In contrast with having polynomial
point count or knowing generators in cohomology, we may throw out arbitrary closed
subsets and still preserve CKgP and generators in Chow.
(4) If X is smooth and proper and has the CKgP, then the cycle class map is an isomor-
phism. In other words — no matter what techniques from Chow we have used in (2)
and (3) — so long as we put our pieces back together into a smooth, proper space,
we now also have access to cohomology.
Part (1) above naturally leads us prove results for the collection of Mg,n that satisfy
2g + n ≤ a for some a (a triangular region bounded by the axes and a line of slope −2).
From this perspective, one should hope to prove A∗(Mg,n) = R∗(Mg,n) for all 2 g + n ≤ 12
(the largest such triangular region that does not contain M1,11). Theorem 1.4 confirms this
hope, but also provides some answers to Questions 1.1 and 1.2 above this region. Obtaining
filled circles with 2 g + n >12, such as M7, requires some more delicate, ad hoc arguments,
and we view the results in this region as more surprising.

1.5. Outline of the paper. In Section 2, we discuss the basics of the tautological rings.
In Section 3, we discuss the Chow–K¨ unneth generation property, which is a key property for
studying the boundary of Mg,n. In Section 4, we explain the inductive method. In Section
5, we summarize previous results on the moduli of pointed curves of genus 0 and 1. In
Section 6, we recall results from [14] to deal with the hyperelliptic locus (which completes
everything in genus 2). In Section 7, we give a quotient stack presentation for certain stacks
of pointed plane curves (which can be used to complete the cases in genus 3). In Section 8, we
introduce the Faber–Pandharipande–Hurwitz cycles, which are tautological cycles associated
to certain finite covers of P1. In Sections 9 and 10, we show that the Chow rings of certain
loci of pointed trigonal and tetragonal curves are generated by these cycles.
